# 2. Three regions, one big divide
## How the North, South, and West made a living

### Regional economies
| Region | Economy | What it wanted |
| North | Factories, trade, and wage labor | Tariffs (import taxes) to protect its factories |
| South | Plantations, cash crops (especially cotton), and enslaved labor | Low tariffs and the right to take slavery west |
| West | Family farms and livestock | Roads and canals to reach markets |

**Sectionalism:** Loyalty to your own region over the country as a whole. Different economies created different interests.

### Cotton and slavery (class notes)
**1790:** About 700,000 enslaved people produced 1,000 tons of cotton.
**1793:** Eli Whitney's cotton gin made it fast to clean cotton, so growing it became far more profitable.
**1860:** The census counted **3,953,760 enslaved people nationwide**. The class notes estimate cotton production at about 1 million tons and show why cotton was crucial to Southern exports. Britain was a major buyer.
**"Cotton is King":** Southerners believed the world depended on their cotton, so no one would dare fight them.
**The result:** The cotton gin did not reduce slavery. It made enslaved labor more valuable and pushed slavery west.

### Anti-slavery activity
**1807:** Congress banned importing enslaved people (in effect from 1808). Smuggling continued, and the domestic slave trade grew.
**Late 1700s-early 1800s:** Northern states passed laws to end slavery, many gradually. A few people stayed enslaved in New Jersey until 1865.
**1833:** William Lloyd Garrison helped found the American Anti-Slavery Society.

### Free Black Americans before the war
Free Black people were not equal citizens. They had to use **separate facilities**, struggled to find jobs, often faced restrictions on voting and testifying in court. The rules varied by state.

# 3. Life under slavery
## Numbers and first-hand accounts from class

### Handout 33: New Perspectives on Slavery
| Question | Answer from the charts |
| States more than 50% Black, 1860 | **South Carolina** (58%) and **Mississippi** (55%) |
| Lowest share of Black residents, 1860 | **Missouri** (10%) and **Delaware** (19%) |
| Southern white families owning no slaves | About **1,150,000** (the chart is for 1860) |
| Life expectancy, 1850 | Free white American **40** years; enslaved American **36** years |
| Most common number owned | **2-4** on the 1860 bar chart; **73%** of owners held 1-9 (1850 pie chart) |
| Owners of 50 or more, 1850 | **3%** |
| Enslaved mothers whose first child came before age 15 | About **4%** |
| Share of Black Americans enslaved | **89.2%** in 1800 and **89.0%** in 1860, down 0.2 percentage points. The number rose from about 894,000 to 3.95 million |
| Share of Black Americans free, 1860 | **11%**, or 488,070 people |

**Big idea:** Most Southern whites owned no slaves, and most owners held only a few. A small group of planters held most enslaved people and most of the power.

### The Middle Passage: three sources
**Middle Passage:** The forced voyage of captive Africans across the Atlantic to the Americas.
**Doc C, Alexander Falconbridge:** A ship's surgeon in the 1780s who later worked with abolitionists. He saw the trade first-hand, but he wrote to turn readers against it. He describes people chained in pairs, packed tightly, sick with **flux** (deadly diarrhea), and threatened with hot coals for refusing food. He says surgeons took the job because they needed the money.
**Doc D, the Brookes diagram:** Shows how people were packed under Britain's Regulated Slave Trade Act of 1788. Even under the law, the ship could hold 454 people. Before the law, it reportedly carried as many as 609. Abolitionists used it as evidence.
**Doc E, Olaudah Equiano:** In his 1789 autobiography, Equiano recalled being kidnapped in West Africa. His birthplace is disputed. He bought his freedom and became an abolitionist. His descriptions of coercion can be compared with Falconbridge’s account and the Brookes diagram. Captives who refused food were flogged; crews needed them alive to sell.

### Daily life (Accounts of Slavery chart)
**Living:** Basic rations, coarse clothing and poor housing were common. The class account describes a monthly ration of 8 pounds of pork and 1 bushel of cornmeal.
**Family:** Children were often separated from their mothers when very young. Families could be sold apart.
**Work:** Long hours under overseers; whipping as punishment.
**Leisure:** Little free time, mostly spent washing, mending, and cooking.

# 4. The road to war, 1820-1857
## Each compromise settled one fight and started the next

| Year and event | What happened | Why it mattered |
| 1820: Missouri Compromise | Maine entered free in 1820 and Missouri entered slave in 1821. Slavery was banned in the rest of the Louisiana Purchase north of 36°30′. | Kept the Senate balanced, 12 free and 12 slave states. |
| 1828-1833: Tariff and Nullification Crisis | The 1828 "Tariff of Abominations" raised import taxes. Calhoun argued a state could **nullify** (cancel) a federal law. South Carolina tried it; President Jackson threatened force. | A compromise tariff ended the crisis, but the states' rights argument survived. |
| 1846: Wilmot Proviso | Proposed banning slavery in land won from Mexico. | Passed the House, failed in the Senate. Showed a North-South split. |
| 1850: Compromise of 1850 | California free; Utah and New Mexico decide by **popular sovereignty**; Texas border and debt settled; slave trade (not slavery) ended in Washington, D.C.; a stronger **Fugitive Slave Act**. | Bought time, but the Fugitive Slave Act angered many Northerners. |
| 1852: Uncle Tom's Cabin | Harriet Beecher Stowe's novel showed slavery's cruelty. | Turned many Northern readers against slavery. |
| 1854: Kansas-Nebraska Act | Stephen Douglas's law let Kansas and Nebraska settlers vote on slavery. | Overturned the 36°30′ line there. Led to "Bleeding Kansas" and the new Republican Party. |
| 1857: Dred Scott v. Sandford | Scott sued for freedom because he had lived in free areas. The Court said he was still enslaved, Black Americans were not citizens, and Congress could not ban slavery in territories. | Made the Missouri Compromise line unconstitutional. Outraged the North. |

**Fugitive Slave Act (1850):** Required people in free states to help return people who had escaped slavery. Accused people could not testify for themselves or get a jury trial.
**Popular sovereignty:** Letting settlers in a territory vote on whether to allow slavery.
**Was the Missouri Compromise a good solution?** In the short run, yes: it kept the Senate balanced and the peace for about 30 years. In the long run, no: it only postponed the fight, and the Kansas-Nebraska Act and Dred Scott decision undid it.

### Main cause behind each event (class notes sheet)
E = economic, S = social, P = political power, W = western expansion. Strongest first.
**Regional economies:** E • **Missouri Compromise:** P, then W • **Nullification:** E, then P • **Abolition:** S
**Wilmot Proviso and 1850:** W, then P • **Fugitive Slave Act:** S, then P • **Kansas-Nebraska:** W, then P • **Dred Scott:** P, then S, then W

# 5. Abolition and two points of view
## Who fought slavery, and how each section saw it

### Abolition leaders
**Abolitionist:** A person who worked to end slavery.
**William Lloyd Garrison:** White Massachusetts editor. His newspaper The Liberator demanded immediate abolition.
**Frederick Douglass:** Escaped slavery, wrote his life story, gave powerful speeches, and published the newspaper North Star.
**Sojourner Truth:** Escaped slavery; spoke for abolition and women's rights.
**Harriet Tubman:** Escaped slavery, then returned South many times to lead others to freedom on the Underground Railroad.
**Angelina and Sarah Grimké:** Sisters from a South Carolina slaveholding family who spoke and wrote against slavery.
**Harriet Beecher Stowe:** Wrote Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852).

### African American contributions
Formerly enslaved people gave the movement its strongest evidence: their own stories. Douglass, Truth, and Tubman spoke, wrote, published, and guided escapes. Free Black communities ran much of the Underground Railroad.

### Underground Railroad
A secret network of routes, guides ("conductors"), and safe houses that helped enslaved people escape to free states or Canada. The class reading estimates that more than 40,000 people received help in Ohio from 1830 to 1860. The network's secrecy makes totals hard to verify.

### The North's view (class reading)
Garrison's approach was combative: end slavery now. By 1840 there were about 2,000 local antislavery societies with perhaps 200,000 members. Still, most Northerners were not abolitionists. Many simply wanted slavery kept out of the West, where they hoped to farm.

### The South's view (class reading)
Only a minority of white Southerners owned slaves. In 1860, 46,274 planters owned at least 20 enslaved people each. Most common farmers owned none, yet they defended slavery: it raised their social standing, and they feared competing with freed Black workers. Southern leaders called slavery a "positive good" and needed new land because cotton wore out the soil.

# 6. 1860: election and secession
## Lincoln wins, and the South leaves

### Lincoln-Douglas debates, 1858
Lincoln said slavery was wrong and must not spread. He argued Black Americans had the natural rights named in the Declaration. Douglas backed popular sovereignty.

### Four candidates in 1860
| Candidate and party | Position |
| Abraham Lincoln, Republican | Stop slavery from spreading into the territories. **Not** abolition where it already existed. |
| Stephen Douglas, Northern Democrat | Popular sovereignty: let settlers vote. |
| John C. Breckinridge, Southern Democrat | Protect slavery in all territories. |
| John Bell, Constitutional Union | Preserve the Union and the Constitution; avoid the slavery issue. |

**Result:** The Democrats split, and Lincoln won the electoral vote with support almost entirely from free states. He was not even on the ballot in most of the South.

### Secession
**Secede:** To formally withdraw from the Union.
**First seven, after the election:** South Carolina (December 20, 1860), Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Texas.
**Four more, after Fort Sumter (April 1861):** Virginia, Arkansas, North Carolina, Tennessee. Total: **11 Confederate states**.
**Why they left:** They believed Lincoln and the Republicans would restrict slavery, and with it their economy and way of life. They claimed states' rights allowed them to leave.
**Confederate States of America:** Formed in Montgomery, Alabama, in February 1861; Jefferson Davis became president; the capital later moved to Richmond, Virginia.
**President Buchanan:** Said secession was illegal but believed he had no power to stop it.

### Know the map (Divided Union, 1863)
**Confederacy (11):** SC, MS, FL, AL, GA, LA, TX, VA, AR, NC, TN.
**Four original slaveholding border states:** Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky and Missouri stayed in the Union. **West Virginia** broke away from Virginia and joined in 1863 with a gradual-emancipation provision. It is sometimes counted as a fifth border state after admission.
**Free Union states (by 1863):** Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut, New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Michigan, Wisconsin, Minnesota, Iowa, Kansas, California, Oregon.
**Territories:** Washington, Dakota, Nebraska, Colorado, Utah, Nevada, New Mexico, and Indian Territory. Nevada became a state in 1864.

# 7. The Civil War, 1861-1865
## From Fort Sumter to Appomattox

### Leaders
| Side | President | Leading general |
| Union (USA) | Abraham Lincoln | Ulysses S. Grant (commander of all Union armies from 1864) |
| Confederacy (CSA) | Jefferson Davis | Robert E. Lee (also Stonewall Jackson, Joseph Johnston) |

### Key events
| Date and event | What happened and why it mattered |
| April 12, 1861: Fort Sumter | Confederates fired on the federal fort in Charleston Harbor after Lincoln announced a resupply mission. The war began, and the North united. |
| July 21, 1861: Bull Run (Manassas) | First major battle, about 3 months after Sumter, 25 miles from Washington, D.C. The South won. Both sides saw the war would be long. |
| September 17, 1862: Antietam | Bloodiest single day of the war. Lee's invasion was stopped, giving Lincoln the chance to announce emancipation. |
| January 1, 1863: Emancipation Proclamation | Declared enslaved people free in designated areas in rebellion. It added emancipation as a Union war aim and authorized Black military service. |
| July 1-3, 1863: Gettysburg | Three-day battle in Pennsylvania. Lee's second invasion of the North failed. **The turning point.** |
| July 4, 1863: Vicksburg | The day after Gettysburg ended, Vicksburg surrendered. With Port Hudson's fall on July 9, the Union controlled the Mississippi River and cut the Confederacy in two. |
| November 19, 1863: Gettysburg Address | Lincoln dedicated the soldiers' cemetery at Gettysburg. |
| 1864: Atlanta and Sherman's March to the Sea | Sherman took Atlanta (September 2). Lincoln won re-election (November 8). Then Sherman's army marched to Savannah (November-December), destroying railroads and supplies. |
| April 9, 1865: Appomattox Court House | The South had run out of food, shoes, uniforms, and ammunition. Lee surrendered his army to Grant in Virginia. The war was effectively over; the other Confederate armies surrendered in the following weeks. |

**Sumter detail:** Lincoln notified South Carolina of a plan to send food to the garrison. Confederate forces opened fire before supplies reached the fort.
**Issue at stake:** States' rights versus federal power, and above all slavery.

# 8. Why the North won
## Advantages, the Emancipation Proclamation, and Gettysburg

### Advantages and disadvantages
| Side | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| North (USA) | Several times the South's population; most factories and war materials; better railroads, canals, and roads; a stronger navy; a recognized government that could get foreign loans | Had to invade and conquer a huge region; long supply lines; weak generals early in the war |
| South (CSA) | Fought on home ground and only had to survive; a strong military tradition; great generals such as Lee, Johnston, and Stonewall Jackson | Fewer people and factories; fewer railroads; no foreign recognition; feared enslaved people would rebel or help the North |

### The North's plan to win
**1.** Blockade Southern ports to cut off trade and smugglers. **2.** Defeat the Confederate army. **3.** Destroy the South's ability to make supplies. **4.** Keep Britain and France from recognizing the Confederacy.

### Who won and why
**The Union won.** Its people, factories, railroads, and navy outlasted the South. Grant pressed on without stopping. Gettysburg and Vicksburg in July 1863 turned the tide. The blockade and Sherman's march wore the South down.

### Emancipation Proclamation, January 1, 1863
**What it did:** Declared enslaved people free in areas still **in rebellion** against the Union.
**Limits:** It exempted loyal slave states and certain Union-held areas. Its practical reach grew as Union armies advanced and enslaved people escaped to Union lines. The **13th Amendment** abolished slavery nationwide, with a punishment-for-crime exception.
**Why it mattered:** Made emancipation a declared war aim alongside preserving the Union. Almost 200,000 Black soldiers and sailors fought for the Union by the war's end.
**Lincoln's goal:** When elected, Lincoln was focused on preserving the Union, not ending slavery. Emancipation became a weapon of war, called a military necessity.

### Gettysburg Address, November 19, 1863
**"Fourscore and seven years ago":** 87 years before 1863 = 1776, the Declaration of Independence. He repeats "all men are created equal."
**"Unfinished work":** Preserving the Union and carrying forward the promise of freedom and equality.
**Words:** consecrate / hallow = make sacred; detract = take away from; devotion = commitment; last full measure of devotion = giving one's life; resolve = firmly decide; in vain = for nothing; perish = die.
**What he asks:** The living must carry on the work so the dead "shall not have died in vain."
**"A new birth of freedom":** A Union rebuilt closer to equality, without slavery. Government "of the people, by the people, for the people" must survive.

# 9. Reconstruction: three plans
## How should the South rejoin the Union?

**Reconstruction (1865-1877):** The period of rebuilding the South and bringing the Confederate states back into the Union. Its central questions: how to reunite the country, how to treat former Confederates, and what rights about 4 million freed people would have.

### April 1865
**April 9:** Lee surrenders at Appomattox; the war is effectively over.
**April 14:** John Wilkes Booth shoots Lincoln at Ford's Theatre in Washington.
**April 15:** Lincoln dies. Vice President **Andrew Johnson** becomes president.

### Comparing the plans
| Plan | Main ideas |
| Lincoln: Ten Percent Plan (1863) | Lenient. When 10% of a state's 1860 voters swore loyalty and accepted emancipation, the state could form a new government. Most Southerners would be pardoned, except high Confederate leaders. |
| Radical Republicans: Wade-Davis Bill (1864) | Strict. A majority had to swear loyalty, and only those who had never supported the Confederacy could take part. Lincoln blocked it with a pocket veto. |
| Johnson's plan (1865) | Lenient, like Lincoln's. States had to accept the 13th Amendment, swear loyalty, and cancel war debts. Johnson pardoned many Confederate leaders, who returned to power and passed Black Codes. |

### Radical Republicans
**Who:** Republicans in Congress led by Thaddeus Stevens and Charles Sumner.
**Ideas:** Punish Confederate leaders, protect freed people's rights, give Black men the vote, and put Congress, not the president, in charge of Reconstruction.
**How they differed from Lincoln:** Lincoln wanted a quick, forgiving reunion. The Radicals wanted a slower, stricter process that changed Southern society.

### Andrew Johnson and impeachment
**Who:** A Democrat from Tennessee who stayed loyal to the Union. As president, he vetoed bills to protect freed people and fought Congress.
**Impeach:** The House formally charges an official with wrongdoing. The Senate then holds a trial; removal requires a two-thirds vote.
**What happened:** The House impeached Johnson on February 24, 1868, mainly for firing Secretary of War Edwin Stanton against the Tenure of Office Act. On May 16, 1868, the Senate voted 35-19 to convict: **one vote short** of two-thirds. Johnson stayed in office.

# 10. Congress steps in
## Laws and amendments to protect freed people

### After the war
Freed people had freedom but little else: no land, little money, and few schools. Many searched for family members who had been sold away. Many former Confederates wanted to keep them in a near-slave position.

### What Congress did
| Year | Action | What it did |
| 1865 | Freedmen's Bureau | Gave food, clothing, and medicine to freed people and refugees; ran schools. |
| 1866 | Civil Rights Act | Made citizens of all colors equal before the law. Aimed at the Black Codes. Passed over Johnson's veto. |
| 1867 | Military Reconstruction Act | Split ten former Confederate states (not Tennessee) into five military districts. To rejoin, a state had to write a constitution letting Black men vote and ratify the 14th Amendment. |
| 1871 | Ku Klux Klan Acts | Let the federal government use the military to stop KKK terror. |

### The Reconstruction Amendments
| Amendment | Text (shortened) | In plain words |
| 13th (1865) | "Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime ... shall exist within the United States." | Slavery is abolished, except as punishment for a crime. |
| 14th (1868) | "All persons born or naturalized in the United States ... are citizens." No state may deny "life, liberty, or property, without due process of law" or "the equal protection of the laws." | People born or naturalized in the United States and subject to its jurisdiction are citizens. States must give all persons due process and equal protection. |
| 15th (1870) | The right to vote "shall not be denied ... on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude." | States cannot deny the vote because of race or past slavery. It did **not** give women the vote. |

### Results
Black men voted and held office. In 1870, Hiram Revels of Mississippi became the first Black U.S. senator. Nearly 500,000 Black voters in the South helped elect Ulysses S. Grant in 1868.

# 11. The South pushes back
## Getting around Reconstruction, and how it ended

| Method | What it was | How it hurt Black Americans |
| Black Codes (1865-1866) | Southern state laws limiting freed people | Banned serving on juries and limited owning or renting land. Unemployed Black people could be arrested for "vagrancy" and forced to work. |
| Sharecropping | A landowner supplied land, tools, and seed; the farmer paid with a **share of the crop** | High prices and debts to the landowner trapped families in poverty, close to slavery. |
| Tenant farming | A farmer **rented** land, paying cash or a set amount of crop, and usually had own tools | A little more independence, but still often in debt. |
| Ku Klux Klan (KKK) | A white supremacist group, founded in 1865-1866 | Used terror, beatings, and murder to stop Black people from voting and to drive out Republicans. |
| Literacy test | A reading test required to vote | Unfairly given to Black voters, many denied schooling under slavery. |
| Poll tax | A fee to vote | Many poor Black voters could not pay. |
| Grandfather clause | Exempted men from tests and taxes if their grandfathers could vote before 1867 | Let poor white men vote while blocking Black men, whose grandfathers had been enslaved. |
| Gerrymandering | Drawing district lines to help one group | Split or packed Black voters to weaken their power. |
| Jim Crow laws | State and local segregation laws, mainly after Reconstruction | Separated Black people in schools, trains, restaurants, and more. |
| Segregation | Separating people by race | Kept Black Americans in worse, unequal facilities. In 1896 (Plessy v. Ferguson) the Supreme Court allowed "separate but equal." |

**Key point:** Literacy tests, poll taxes, and grandfather clauses got around the 15th Amendment without naming race.

### How Reconstruction ended
**Northern interest faded:** Economic troubles and years of conflict wore down Northern support.
**Southern Democrats returned to power** state by state, often through violence and fraud.
**Compromise of 1877:** The 1876 election was disputed. Democrats accepted Republican **Rutherford B. Hayes** as president; in return, Hayes pulled federal troops away from protecting the last Republican state governments in the South (South Carolina and Louisiana).
**Result:** Without protection, many gains were lost. The 15th Amendment went largely unenforced, and the Jim Crow era began.

# 12. Review-sheet answers: 1-6
## Short answers to the teacher's questions

### 1. On what were the regional economies based?
**North:** Factories, trade, and wage labor. **South:** Plantations and cash crops, especially cotton, worked by enslaved people. **West:** Family farms and livestock; wanted roads and canals.

### 2. Was the Missouri Compromise a good solution?
**Yes, in the short run:** It kept the Senate balanced and the peace for about 30 years.
**No, in the long run:** It only postponed the problem. Each new territory reopened the fight. The Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854) overturned its line, and the Dred Scott decision (1857) called such bans unconstitutional.

### 3. What were living conditions like for most slaves?
Harsh. Basic rations (about 8 pounds of pork and 1 bushel of cornmeal a month), coarse clothing, and poor housing. Long hours under overseers, whipping, and families sold apart. Life expectancy in 1850: 36 years for enslaved Americans versus 40 for free whites.

### 4. Who led the abolition movement? What did African Americans contribute?
**Leaders:** Garrison (The Liberator), Frederick Douglass, Sojourner Truth, Harriet Tubman, the Grimké sisters, Harriet Beecher Stowe.
**African American contributions:** They gave first-hand proof of slavery's cruelty through speeches, autobiographies, and newspapers (Douglass's North Star). They led escapes (Tubman) and ran much of the Underground Railroad.

### 5. What were the major points of the Compromise of 1850?
California entered as a free state. Utah and New Mexico would decide slavery by popular sovereignty. Texas's border and debt were settled. The slave trade ended in Washington, D.C. A stronger Fugitive Slave Act required free states to return people who escaped slavery.

### 6. What were the effects of the Kansas-Nebraska Act?
It let settlers vote on slavery and overturned the Missouri Compromise line there. Proslavery and antislavery settlers rushed into Kansas, and violence broke out ("Bleeding Kansas"). Anger over the act led to the new Republican Party, which opposed slavery's spread.

# 14. Review-sheet answers: 12-17
## Short answers to the teacher's questions

### 12. Radical Republicans
**Who:** Republicans in Congress, led by Thaddeus Stevens and Charles Sumner.
**Ideas:** Punish Confederate leaders, protect freed people, give Black men the vote, and let Congress control Reconstruction.
**Versus Lincoln:** Lincoln's Ten Percent Plan was quick and forgiving. The Radicals wanted a stricter plan that changed Southern society.

### 13. Lincoln, Johnson, and impeachment
**Lincoln:** Shot by John Wilkes Booth on April 14, 1865; died April 15.
**Johnson:** Lincoln's vice president, a Southern Democrat loyal to the Union. He became president and gave the South a lenient plan.
**Impeachment:** The House charges an official; the Senate holds a trial. The House impeached Johnson in 1868. The Senate fell one vote short of removing him.

### 14. African Americans after the war; Congress; the amendments
**After the war:** Freedom, but little land or money, and attacks from Black Codes and the KKK.
**Congress helped:** The Freedmen's Bureau (food, schools), the Civil Rights Act of 1866, the Military Reconstruction Act of 1867, and the KKK Acts of 1871.
**13th:** Ended slavery. **14th:** Citizenship and equal protection of the laws. **15th:** No denying the vote because of race.

### 15. How did the South respond? How did it get around Reconstruction?
Black Codes, KKK violence, and sharecropping kept freed people poor and controlled. Later, literacy tests, poll taxes, and grandfather clauses kept Black men from voting without naming race, and gerrymandering weakened the votes they did cast. Jim Crow laws segregated public life.

### 16. How did Reconstruction end?
Northern support faded. In the Compromise of 1877, Democrats accepted Hayes as president. In return, he stopped using federal troops to protect the last Republican governments in the South.
